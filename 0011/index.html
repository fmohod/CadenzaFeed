From the Ashes We Rise: The Houston Fashion Summit Debuts With Runway, Panels and Purpose

By Frankie Mohammed 8 min read Filed under: Culture

The inaugural Houston Fashion Summit turned a historic Near Northside room into a survivor-centered showcase — a runway, three panels and a vendor market, with half the proceeds pledged to two local nonprofits.

HOUSTON — Red, orange and yellow balloons arched over the runway as guests settled into rows of chairs inside The Event Place, a restored brick room on James Street in the Near Northside. On Saturday evening, July 18, designers, panelists, nonprofit leaders and local entrepreneurs gathered there for the first Houston Fashion Summit — an ambitious hybrid of runway show, industry panel and pop-up market built around a single, deliberately hopeful idea, carried across the summit's materials and echoed through the night: From the Ashes We Rise — Honoring the Power of Fashion.

The summit billed itself as a gathering that "celebrates the arts, diversity, and inclusion rooted on survivorship" — a framing that set it apart from the usual competitive showcase and gave the evening its purpose.

That purpose belonged to Perla Lugo, the summit's founder and creative director, who produced the event through her company, Sugar Pearl Productions. Lugo, a working model who describes her business as fashion-show and model coordination "for non-profits and small businesses," assembled a planning committee of ambassadors drawn from across Houston's creative and small-business community to build the first edition — labeled "Issue 01" on the designers' own announcement cards. The night was, by that billing, a debut, and it carried a debut's sense of ambition: the summit's materials described it, without understatement, as "the future of fashion in Houston."

The format was broad by design. Running from six to nine, the evening braided together four things a fashion event rarely attempts at once: a set of educational Q&A panels, a runway of local designers and boutiques, a vendor market of small businesses, and a nonprofit fundraiser. The event's online RSVP list — capped at eighty — had filled before the doors opened, and the room, one of the larger event spaces in the Near Northside, drew a seated audience that flanked a single straight runway.

The venue itself was part of the message. The Event Place, at 508 James Street, is a restored industrial room the summit's team praised as "a historic space reborn through community revival" — co-owned by one of the summit's own ambassadors, Kat Moon, who also sits on the board of the Greater Northside Chamber of Commerce. Decor company DB Decorations, run by a vendor the summit credited simply as Brenda, framed the stage with a tall archway of red, orange and yellow balloons that recurs behind nearly every runway frame from the night — a warm, sunset-colored backdrop that suited the from-the-ashes theme. The Pink Macaron, a micro-venue out in Rosenberg, was listed as an in-kind sponsor.

On the runway

The show moved down one straight runway toward a wall of photographers at the far end, with hosts working microphones at the front to introduce each label. The designer lineup leaned local and varied. Flowers by Linda Reyes, credited as a floral fashion designer, produced some of the evening's most photographed looks: a model in a flowing white dress and a large floral headpiece, and another walk carried out with an armful of fresh blooms. Angela Rose Wearable Art — the brand of designer Angela Rose, who has spoken about beginning her design career in her mid-forties — brought the wearable-art sensibility its name promises. Lopez Atelier, Gin Martini Designs, Kira Alexander, The Freelance Slayers (the label of published designer-stylist Mika Champagne), and the collective Womenace Society rounded out the runway names.

Frankie Mohammed's photographs from the floor catch the range of the styling. One model stands against the balloon arch in a shimmering bronze outfit patterned with stars; another poses in a silver dress layered over a blue sequined skirt; a third works the runway in a leopard-print dress with fringed sleeves, microphone in hand. There are elegant restraint pieces — a black strapless gown finished with short gloves — and there is play: a bright, patterned swimwear look, a pink crop top over cut-out leggings, a small frilly handbag held just so. Boutiques carried a share of the racks, with Kaylen's Boutique, Miranda's Boutique, Siren Sisters Boutique and Haus of Electric Love all on the bill, and the jewelry was its own category: Brambelii, Glareta Jewelry and J. Aimer Jewelry each presented at the summit. (Because the summit's model roster was not published, individual models are described here by their looks rather than named.)

The conversations

If the runway was the summit's body, the panels were its argument. Three separate Q&A sessions, moderated by host and content creator Nicole Napper (who works under the handle @thefirekitty and founded Houston Hotties Social Hour), gave the evening its stated reason for being. The first, Powerful Women in Fashion, gathered creative consultant and event curator Angela Adams, editorial and brand photographer Yeni Martinez, Roxann Gutierrez, and beauty-and-health entrepreneur Jessica Benitez for a conversation the summit framed around brand-building, entrepreneurship and leadership. The second panel, Fashion as a Tool for Healing: A Survivor Story, featured Tina Johnson and Laura Castro, and pulled the survivorship theme from subtext into text. The third, For the Love of Fashion: Industry Experts, paired designer Angela Rose with Brent Law to talk shop about the business behind the clothes.

Taken together, the panels made the case that the summit wanted the runway to illustrate: that fashion in Houston is an industry and a livelihood, not only a spectacle, and that the same craft can double as a way through hard seasons.

Fashion with a purpose

The night's fundraising was explicit rather than incidental. The summit pledged half of its proceeds to two Houston nonprofits. The first, the Hearts of Grace Foundation, is a 501(c)(3) that provides care packages, warm meals, mentorship, scholarships and community programs to the elderly, youth, veterans, single mothers and people experiencing homelessness. It was established by Perla Rodriguez — a summit ambassador and, despite the shared first name, a different person from founder Perla Lugo — in memory of her mother, Maria Luisa Rodriguez, whom the foundation's statement remembers as "a woman whose life radiated grace, kindness and boundless compassion." The second beneficiary, The Transparent Closet, operates as a free clothing boutique for transitioning youth, teens and young adults, offering essentials in a safe shopping environment. Presenting the two side by side, the summit drew a straight line from the runway to the neighborhood: clothing as dignity, and fashion as a platform for empowerment and healing.

Beyond the runway

Some of the evening's warmth came from its edges. Ringing the room, the Trendsetters Market — the pop-up arm of ambassador Alexandra Mendez's small-business venture — hosted eleven vendors, a cross-section of Houston enterprise: jewelry from Brambelii, sweets from Xo's Sweet Creations, coffee from Amorcito Cafe, holistic wellness from Serenity Esthetics & More, cocktails from Klassy Kocktails, plus Create Action TX, Siren Sisters Boutique, Glareta Jewelry, Womenace Society and the Gio's Purpose Foundation, a scholarship nonprofit that jewelry brand Brambelii funds in memory of its founder's brother, lost to gun violence.

And then there was Sammy. Samuel — who goes by Sammy, and by the handle @sammy_on_the_streets — is a small, black-and-tan rescue dog found on the streets of Waco six years ago and now the face of his own treat company, Sammy's Sweet Treat. Perched in a polka-dotted bed at his vendor table amid the balloons and snacks, Sammy became an unexpected favorite of the night; he appears throughout the photographer's coverage, and his owner walked Mohammed through the product with the easy pride of a small-business owner whose four-legged companion had quietly become one of the night's most photographed personalities. Outside, in the lot, two guests in elaborate, layered costume-couture posed by the parked cars — the kind of self-styled attendee who treats a fashion night as an invitation to become part of the show.

From the floor

Covering the runway from two positions along its length, Mohammed noted an evening that ran at an unhurried pace. Models held their marks and the walks were measured, which gave photographers room to work but kept the room's energy lower-key than a peak-hour showcase; the crowd, seated in a comfortable, air-conditioned space, watched more than it roared. The entrance and bar sat close together near the door, concentrating the room's foot traffic at one end. These observations reflect the natural rhythm of a first-year event establishing its identity — notes on flow and atmosphere, not verdicts on the work, which was earnest and, in the floral and wearable-art pieces especially, genuinely crafted.

Why it mattered

Independent fashion events often receive little lasting documentation despite their importance to local creative communities. By photographing and archiving events like the Houston Fashion Summit, Cadenza Arthouse aims to preserve Houston's evolving cultural history and keep a permanent public record for the designers, models, artists, nonprofits and organizers who build it.

For all the moving parts, the Houston Fashion Summit was legible in its intent. It set out to put local designers, boutiques and jewelers in front of a paying audience; to turn that attention into money for two neighborhood nonprofits; and to insist, through its panels and its theme, that fashion can be a vehicle for survivorship and community rather than competition. As a debut it was inevitably a work in progress. But in a city whose fashion scene runs largely on independent effort, an event that pairs a runway with a fundraiser and a set of frank industry conversations — and gives its ambassadors, vendors and a street-rescue dog equal room to shine — is exactly the kind of thing worth documenting the first time it happens. If the summit's own promise holds, July 18 was only Issue 01.
