Libraries Are Doing Everything Now

By Staff Reporter 7 min read Filed under: Infrastructure & Society

On a Tuesday morning at the Kenton County Public Library in northern Kentucky, the following things are happening simultaneously: a retired teacher is helping a man in his fifties fill out a Medicaid renewal form. A social worker is meeting with a mother about emergency housing vouchers. A librarian is running a workshop on how to spot scam phone calls, attended mostly by people in their seventies. Three teenagers are doing homework. And in the lobby, behind a small glass case, there are free doses of naloxone — no questions, no forms, just take one if you need it.

This is a library in 2026. It is also a social services hub, a digital equity center, a safe warm space, a de facto day shelter, a job training site, and the only place in a fifteen-mile radius where a person without a home address can receive mail. It is all of these things on a budget that, in real terms, has declined in most American cities over the past two decades.

The Last Truly Public Space

Sociologists have a term for places where people of different backgrounds can occupy the same space without a commercial transaction being required: third places. The pub, the town square, the park. Most American cities have spent the last fifty years systematically defunding or privatizing their third places. The library endured. It is, in many communities, the only institution left that will let you sit inside all day without buying anything.

That openness, once a philosophical commitment, has become a logistical challenge of significant proportions. Libraries in cities with severe homelessness crises have quietly become de facto shelters — not because anyone decided they should be, but because they are warm, open, and non-coercive. Staff who trained to help children find picture books now receive de-escalation training and carry naloxone on their lanyards.

"I didn't get a master's in library science to do triage. But I also don't know who else was going to."
— Public librarian, Cincinnati branch

The librarians navigating this expanded role are, by almost any account, doing it thoughtfully and often heroically. They are also burning out at rates that administrators are only beginning to acknowledge. A survey by the American Library Association found that more than 60 percent of public-facing library staff reported significantly increased stress related to patron mental health crises in the past three years. A quarter said they were considering leaving the profession.

What Libraries Were Built For — and What That Means Now

The original public library ideal — democratic, free, universal access to information and knowledge — was radical when Andrew Carnegie funded hundreds of them at the turn of the twentieth century. It remains radical. A physical space, open to everyone, staffed by trained professionals, where the only requirement is treating the space and other patrons with basic respect. No fee. No membership. No loyalty card.

That ideal hasn't changed. What's changed is the depth of need surrounding it. When other public institutions contract — when social services are cut, when public spaces disappear, when the safety net develops holes large enough to fall through — people end up at the library. Because the library is still there. Because the library still says yes.

The question that city councils and state legislatures are slowly, reluctantly beginning to confront is whether the library can keep saying yes without the resources to mean it. The books are still there. The librarians are still there. The mission is still there. Whether the funding follows is, as always, a political choice.

In Kenton County, the Tuesday morning social worker is a contract position, funded by a one-time grant that expires next March. Nobody knows yet what happens after that.
